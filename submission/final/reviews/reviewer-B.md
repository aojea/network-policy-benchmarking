## Paper summary
The paper asks where NetworkPolicy enforcement cost lands when a cluster has many distinct policy identities and high Pod churn. It contrasts eager per-endpoint materialization (Cilium's identity-keyed BPF policy maps, modeled as $2I$ entries per endpoint) with kube-network-policies (KNP), which steers uncached packets of policy-selected Pods to NFQUEUE via nftables, evaluates NetworkPolicy semantics in a userspace agent when a connection is attempted, caches an accept verdict as a conntrack label so the kernel forwards the rest of the connection, and learns local Pod addresses through NRI hooks instead of the API server. Evidence is archived ClusterLoader2 runs on a 720-worker GCE cluster creating 35,000 sandbox Pods at 7/700/3,500/35,000 intended identities; every sandbox carries a `wait-for-gateway` init container that blocks until a permitted TCP connection succeeds under default deny, so `schedule_to_run` upper-bounds time to first permitted communication. Findings: Cilium's P99 falls with identity count in its completed tiers; its 35k bidirectional-mesh tier was skipped by a runner guard on predicted policy-map capacity; KNP is slower than Cilium at every matched tier (0.5–2.4 s at 700/3,500 identities, 22× at 7), completes an unmatched raw-Pod 35k mesh tier with all Pods Running but a 421 s P99 and two startup-SLO failures, and created Pods 2.5–3× more slowly for unrecorded reasons. A security analysis of the fail-open default and a capacity model with one unprovenanced service-rate data point complete the contributions.

## Strengths
- The `wait-for-gateway` gate is a genuinely good methodological idea: folding one policy-gated cross-node connection into every Pod's Running transition makes `schedule_to_run` a stronger quantity than container-start latency, and Section 6.1 states its four caveats explicitly.
- Real scale and exemplary provenance discipline (Appendix A; "never add or average percentiles across components or runs"; a missing report "is treated as missing, never as a zero"). Every number in Tables 1–3 traces to a named file.
- Section 3.3's security analysis (fail-open as a bypass vector with no per-source rate limit or negative cache; unknown-peer deny; shutdown semantics) is candid and correct about consequences.
- Section 7 concedes that the per-peer cross-product is one kernel representation among several (OVS conjunctive match, Calico ipsets) and that the paper lacks a factored-kernel baseline.
- Table 4 and Section 8.2 give a costed, prioritized list of missing experiments with disconfirming outcomes stated in advance.

## Weaknesses
- The only matched comparison (mesh tiers 2–3) is undermined by the authors' own confound: KNP's cluster created Pods at 124/103 Pods/s against Cilium's 302/328 "on the same configured 500 QPS and identical control-plane settings" (Section 6.2, Table 2), which the paper agrees flatters KNP's tail. Section 6.8 shows the gap reversed sign in July (Kindnet 233 vs Cilium 180). No API-server or controller-manager metrics explain it.
- The system under test produced no telemetry: all six KNP agent queries and the kubelet CNI-latency query returned `dataItems: null` in every tier (Table 2, Section 6.9). $\lambda_q$, $\mu$, drops, and agent CPU — the quantities Section 4 turns on — are unmeasured; the only $\mu$ is a 2024 three-node ApacheBench run with unrecorded hardware that hit `nf_conntrack: table full`.
- The headline "completes the excluded tier" (Abstract, Section 8.3) sets an executed raw-Pod run (120 s P50, 421 s P99 after scheduling, two SLO failures) against a tier the benchmark's own `MESH_SWEEPS` list and guard chose not to run (Section 6.5). Nothing was observed about Cilium at 35k mesh identities.
- The capacity exclusion rests on a script header comment, a source default, and inference from completed unidirectional runs; "allocated Cilium identities were not dumped" (Section 6.1) and no `cilium-dbg bpf policy get` was taken (Section 6.5). Even Figure 2's x-axis is unverified.
- Every cell is one trial (Section 6.1). The paper says the 0.5 s and 2.4 s differences "are indistinguishable from noise" (Section 8.2), yet the abstract reports them to three decimals and Section 1 claims KNP's P99 "does not rise from 700 to 3,500."
- The KNP binary and flags are unknown: Kindnet v1.0.1 in the cluster, v1.1.0 in the repository manifest, revision 9984dcd inspected, and "neither image is matched to revision 9984dcd" (Section 5). Whether `--fail-open` was in effect is unarchived.
- Negative enforcement is a one-shot manual smoke test per idle cluster, unarchived (Section 6.1), and "a configured mesh permission does not generate all-to-all traffic" (Section 6.7), so the dense tiers charge the eager engine its cost and the deferred engine nothing.
- The 151 s and 421 s KNP tails fall on the very quantity the design claims to improve, and "nothing in the artifacts explains" them (Section 6.2).

## Detailed comments for authors

1. **Control-plane confound (Section 6.2, Table 2).** "Identical control-plane settings" means identical cluster spec and generator QPS, but Phase-1 throughput here is scheduled Pods/s downstream of the ReplicaSet controller; one RS create fans out to 50 or 10 Pods, so 500 QPS is not the limiter on either cluster. Candidates are controller-manager batching and client QPS, APF queueing, watch-cache pressure, and scheduler binding rate — none instrumented. Your own Section 8.1 supplies the most plausible hypothesis: 720 KNP agents each hold a full Pod informer, so the KNP API server fans out on the order of $NU$ Pod events during the fill. If Cilium's agents impose no equivalent watch load, the slower stream is not an external confound "in KNP's favor" but a cost of the design, and end-to-end throughput becomes the fair metric — on which KNP loses at every tier. Either way the matched-tier conclusion does not survive: per-node arrival rates differed 2.5–3×, and since Section 6.8 shows the sign flipping in July, it is not a stable bias one could correct for. Table 4 already names the fix.

2. **The gate as a proxy (Section 6.1).** `schedule_to_run` bounds first-permitted-communication only if the init container dominates it, and without the `Latency: N ms` lines you cannot show that. The gate hides image pull on first use per node (were images pre-pulled?), serialized sandbox creation and PLEG relist in a kubelet absorbing ~49 Pods, kubelet status-update QPS, and Service programming for the gateway ClusterIP — a dataplane cost unrelated to policy. More seriously, the gate manufactures denied traffic: each pending sandbox retries `nc -z -w 1` about once a second, and every SYN reaching a gateway node before that node has the sandbox's metadata is dropped unlabeled and re-queued on the next attempt (Section 3.1). The gateway nodes therefore see exactly the denied-traffic burst Section 3.3 warns about. Under the fail-open default a full 1,024-packet queue passes the SYN unevaluated and the gate succeeds — a saturated gateway node makes Pods reach Running *sooner*, and "positive enforcement per Pod" is not established either. Please report the gateway Pod/node count and estimate their peak SYN arrival.

3. **Tier 4 (Section 6.2, Table 1).** A 120 s median and 421 s P99 after scheduling, `create_to_schedule` P99 of 107 s, and per-second throughput with median 0 and peak 841 describe a scheduler-limited burst landing on kubelets that then serialize ~49 sandboxes each; your candidate causes say as much. The policy engine's share is unmeasured. "Completes the excluded tier" is an existence proof that no map bound stopped it, and the harness itself failed the run against its 300 s P90 SLO. Since the exclusion was your guard rather than an observed Cilium failure, the informative experiment was to run the Cilium tier and record the failure mode (lockdown, Pods in `Init:0/1` — the symptom of issue 7515 you cite). A run versus a non-run is not a comparison.

4. **Capacity exclusion (Section 6.5, Figure 2 right).** I agree with your own characterization: design evidence, not measurement. The inference "all 35,000 Running in the unidirectional tiers ⇒ effective map ≥ 35,000" also assumes that 35,000 intended label sets became 35,000 allocated identities (never dumped) and that churn across five 5,000-Pod rounds stayed below the 65,280 allocator limit the raw-Pod run exhausted (Section 6.6), while Section 4 says whether churn created fresh identities "is not recorded." Plotting a flag maximum beside measured P99s invites readers to see a wall that was never touched. Acceptable as motivation, not as a result.

5. **Version dependence (Section 6.4).** It cuts against the paper: the baseline is release-sensitive, so the snapshot is fragile, and the methodology cannot attribute failures (counts from a narrative file, no logs). Applied symmetrically, KNP fares worse: unidentified binary, unrecorded flags. The one gain is evidence that the gate detects stranded Pods (19 in `Init:0/1`) — support for the methodology, not the system.

6. **Reproducibility (Table 4, Appendix A).** The provenance appendix is the best I have seen in a submission. But Table 4 lists agent metrics, binary digests, effective flags, abort logs, repeat trials, the map dump, and the throughput cause — everything required to turn observations into findings. I cannot recommend accepting a paper whose system under test has no telemetry and no identified binary, however well the absence is documented.

7. **Operational safety (Section 3.3).** For an operator, an engine whose default under overload admits denied traffic is a policy bypass, and the alternative turns overload into a node-wide outage of managed Pods; with no per-source rate limit a tenant chooses which. Shutdown with fail-open leaves an unenforced window. Unknown-peer deny is correct, and the benchmark commendably measures its availability cost. But the safety property was checked by a hand-run script once per idle cluster, unarchived, the bypass window was never sized, and per comment 2 your own benchmark may have exercised it.

8. **The experiment that would most change my score.** Rerun mesh tiers 2 and 3 on both clusters, three trials each, with the Kindnet image rebuilt to expose agent metrics, `Latency:` lines scraped, API-server and controller-manager metrics collected, digests and flags written to `cl2-metadata.json`, and the generator paced so both clusters achieve the same Pods/s. If KNP stays within a few seconds of Cilium at equal arrival rate with $\rho$ measurably below one and zero queue drops, the matched-tier claim becomes a result and I move to weak accept. Second priority is the fail-open/fail-closed denied-traffic microbenchmark.

9. **Presentation.** The honesty is a credit, but the paper reads as an evidence audit: the abstract spends more words on what is not shown than on what is, and three of the five "observations" in Section 1 concern Cilium or confounds. Readers should not have to do the accounting to discover that the only positive statement is an existence proof. Either collect the Table 4 evidence and write a result paper, or reframe as a methodology paper on benchmarking policy engines at scale (the gate, the throughput confound, fan-out halving at 35,000 ReplicaSets), which the present data supports better than the design-comparison framing.

## Questions for authors' response
1. Do you have `apiserver_flowcontrol_*` or `apiserver_request_duration` series for either cluster during the fill? Do the 720 KNP agents' full Pod watches (Section 8.1) load the API server materially more than Cilium's agents do?
2. How many gateway Pods and nodes served the probes, what was their peak SYN arrival rate, and was `--fail-open` effectively true in the archived runs? Could probes have passed via bypass?
3. Were sandbox images pre-pulled, and what kubelet settings (max-pods, serialize-image-pulls, PLEG) applied? Can any `Latency:` lines be recovered from surviving node logs?
4. Why exclude Cilium's 35k mesh tier rather than run it and record the failure mode?
5. Which image digest ran in the KNP mesh sweep, and is its agent code equivalent to revision 9984dcd on the queue, bypass, and NRI paths?
