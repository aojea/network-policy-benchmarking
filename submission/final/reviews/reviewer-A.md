# Reviewer A

## Overall merit
2 (Weak reject) — An unusually candid paper about a reasonable decomposition, but the evaluation never measures the quantity the design moves (userspace verdict load), the only matched comparison is confounded and single-trial, and the headline capacity result arrives with a 421 s tail and an unexplained 22× regression, so the paper does not yet deliver a result.

## Reviewer expertise
4 (Expert)

## Paper summary
The paper describes kube-network-policies (KNP), a NetworkPolicy enforcer that avoids materializing per-peer-identity state at every endpoint. nftables keeps sets of policy-selected local Pod addresses and steers packets with no cached verdict to NFQUEUE; a userspace agent evaluates NetworkPolicy semantics against Pod/Namespace metadata and, on accept, sets a conntrack label so a kernel rule forwards the rest of the connection. Denied packets are dropped unlabeled. Local Pod addresses come from NRI `RunPodSandbox` callbacks rather than the API watch. Section 4 gives an accounting: an eager engine carries about $dIP_\ell$ entries per node; deferred evaluation removes that term and adds a queue with stability condition $\rho=\lambda_q/\mu<1$. Section 3.3 argues from source that the default `--fail-open=true`, a 1,024-packet queue, and no negative cache for denies form a bypass vector.

The evaluation is a 720-worker, 35,000-Pod ClusterLoader2 sweep comparing Cilium v1.20.1 with Kindnet v1.0.1 (bundling KNP), where every sandbox gates `Running` on a permitted TCP connection to a gateway under default deny. KNP completes a 35,000-identity bidirectional-mesh tier that Cilium's runner excluded on predicted policy-map capacity (421 s P99, two SLO failures). At 700 and 3,500 identities its P99 is within 0.5–2.4 s of Cilium while creating Pods 2.5–3× more slowly; at 7 identities it records 151 s against 6.8 s, unexplained. Agent metrics are absent and each tier is one run. The authors disclaim any latency advantage.

## Strengths
- A real testbed (720 workers, 35,000 Pods) and a `wait-for-gateway` gate that makes `schedule_to_run` bound time-to-first-permitted-connection across nodes, a more meaningful admission metric than container start.
- Exceptional honesty: Section 6.9 and Table 4 enumerate every missing measurement, the abstract disclaims a latency advantage, and the capacity exclusion is framed as analytical, not observed (Section 6.5).
- Section 3.3's reading of fail-open plus unlabeled denies is correct and operationally important.
- Section 7 correctly names OVS conjunctive match, Antrea, and Calico's ipset factoring as the relevant kernel-resident alternatives and admits the missing baseline.
- The Cilium-side failure records (v1.18.6 stranded Pods, raw-Pod abort) are useful community data, though case studies rather than results.

## Weaknesses
1. The thesis is that cost moves to userspace admission, yet $\lambda_q$, $\mu$, queue occupancy, drops, and agent CPU are unmeasured in every run: "all six agent queries returned no samples" because "the deployed Kindnet build exposes no metrics port" (Sections 5, 6.3). The destination of the displaced work is never observed.
2. The only matched comparison (mesh, 700 and 3,500 identities) is confounded: KNP created Pods at 103–124/s versus Cilium's 302–328/s on the same configured QPS (Table 2), which the authors concede "flatters the tail" (Section 8.2). One trial per cell, so the 0.5 s and 2.4 s gaps are "indistinguishable from noise."
3. Tier 1 is a 22× regression on the metric the design targets: 38.946 s P50 / 151.479 s P99 against Cilium's 1.412 / 6.767 s, and "nothing in the artifacts explains it" (Section 6.2). The July tuned bundle reached 9.718 s at the same shape (Section 6.8), so it is unexplained relative to the authors' own prior run too.
4. Tier 4 is unmatched (KNP raw Pods vs. no Cilium mesh run) and carries a 421 s P99, 106 s `create_to_schedule` P99, and two startup-SLO failures. Completion is a `Running` count, not a scaling result.
5. Novelty is not isolated. Evaluate-once-per-connection via conntrack is what OVS/OVN/Antrea do; the actual deltas, userspace semantic evaluation and NRI discovery, are untested in isolation: "no NRI on/off comparison exists" (Section 3.2), no factored-kernel baseline (Section 7).
6. The fail-open analysis is a source reading, not a demonstrated vulnerability; no mitigation is proposed; the benchmark ran with the default, so the positive gate could pass via bypass (Section 6.1, caveat 3). Negative enforcement was checked once per cluster, unarchived.
7. Section 4 is an accounting identity plus $\rho<1$; the only $\mu$ estimate is a 2024 three-node ApacheBench run under `nf_conntrack: table full`.
8. Provenance: the deployed binary is unmatched to the inspected revision and agent flags are unrecorded, so differences are "between stacks, not attributable solely to the code described here" (Section 5).

## Detailed comments for authors
1. **Positioning (a).** Section 7 is the strongest part of the paper and also the most damaging: it concedes that OVS classifies in userspace and caches in the kernel, that Antrea "marks established connections in conntrack so that only new flows walk the policy tables," and that Calico factors selectors into ipsets so per-endpoint state grows with rules rather than peers. nftables sets and verdict maps give the same $n+m$ factoring without leaving the kernel, and Section 2 cites Sutter and the kube-proxy nftables backend for exactly that. So why go to userspace at all? The implicit answer is that interpreting semantics against live metadata avoids selector compilation, but that argument is never made, and the cost it introduces (a per-connection round trip and bypass-on-overflow semantics) is never measured. Comparing against Cilium alone measures one representation choice (a per-identity policy map with a 65,536 ceiling), not "kernel enforcement." The capacity claim in Section 6.5 would likely evaporate against Antrea or Calico, as Section 8.2 itself says. Add that baseline or retitle the contribution as a Cilium-specific capacity observation.

2. **Fail-open (b).** The analysis is correct and I credit it, but it is a liability the paper documents, not a contribution it makes. Denied packets are never labeled, so every retry of a denied SYN re-enters the queue; with `--fail-open` default true and a 1,024-packet queue, a tenant saturates its own node's evaluator with traffic it may not send and the overflow is accepted. That is a policy bypass by design in a policy enforcer. To count as a contribution I would require: (i) the single-node overload experiment listed seventh in Section 8.2, fail-open and fail-closed, reporting the bypass window; (ii) at least one mitigation evaluated: a conntrack deny label so a denied flow is dropped in-kernel thereafter, per-source queue admission, or fail-closed by default; (iii) evidence that the archived positive gate was not itself admitted via bypass. The deny-amplification also interacts with the benchmark: every sandbox's first SYN reaches the gateway node's evaluator before that node has the sandbox's metadata, is dropped, and is retried; the gateway node is a structural hotspot for KNP under this workload, and the paper never discusses it.

3. **Cost model (c).** Equations 1–3 are correct but trivial: state $\propto dIP_\ell$ versus $\rho<1$ is the standard eager-vs-lazy accounting. A model earns its place by predicting something that is then measured; here nothing is. $\lambda_q$ and $\mu$ are unmeasured at 720 nodes, the 2024 microbenchmark's 3,080 ms P99 is a SYN-retransmit signature under conntrack exhaustion, and Equation 2 omits the userspace index cost the paper admits "can dominate." The right panel of Figure 2 plots the model with no measured point. Keep it as one framing paragraph, not a contribution.

4. **Methodology (d).** The gate is a good idea, but as used it cannot support an engine comparison. `schedule_to_run` folds image pull, container start, CNI ADD, the `nc -z -w 1` loop's 1 s quantization, and metadata convergence to the gateway node into one number, and the per-Pod `Latency:` lines that would separate them "were not collected." The throughput confound is fatal to the matched tiers: a node absorbing Pods at 103/s instead of 328/s sees smaller bursts, and the paper cannot say why the Kindnet cluster was slower. Single trials preclude any statistical statement. Tier 4 compares a KNP raw-Pod run to nothing. The empty agent metrics mean the one quantity the design relocates is never seen. The evaluation does not test the claim.

5. **The 151 s result (e).** The paper handles it honestly — it is in the abstract, Table 1, Figure 2, and the conclusion — but honesty is not diagnosis. A 38.9 s median is not a tail effect; the whole distribution shifted. Section 6.2 says it "tracks large-ReplicaSet bursts rather than identity count," yet the July tuned bundle at the same shape achieved 9.7 s, so something regressed between July and September that the paper does not name. Without kubelet PLEG/CNI timings or init-container logs, no one can exclude the deferred-evaluation path itself. A design targeting admission latency cannot leave a 22× regression on that metric as an open item.

6. **What would change my score (f).** In order: (1) rebuild the agent with metrics exposed and rerun tier 1 with `Latency:` lines collected, so the 151 s is attributed; (2) equalize achieved Pod throughput (or explain it from controller-manager metrics) and repeat tiers 2–3 three times each; (3) a factored-kernel baseline (Antrea or Calico nftables) at the 35,000-identity mesh; (4) the fail-open/fail-closed overload microbenchmark with a deny mix. (1)–(2) would move me to weak accept if tier 1 is a confound and the matched tiers hold; (3)–(4) are needed for accept.

7. **Presentation (g).** Figure 2's log axis hides the 2.3–2.9 s vs 2.8–5.3 s differences while connecting lines across tiers that change Pods-per-ReplicaSet imply a trend the paper disclaims; use per-tier CDFs on a linear axis and a separate panel for the burst tiers. Table 1 mixes three Cilium policies with one KNP policy and places an aborted Cilium hub-and-spoke run beside a completed KNP mesh run while the caption says they "are not a matched pair"; split it. Table 2's "0† / 310" P50 is not a throughput number. Section 8.1 spends nearly a page on IPTracker, which "no result in Section 6 used," and Section 6.8 is a tuning chronology; that space should hold the missing measurements. Sections 3–4 lack a timing diagram of the first-packet path (steering, queue wait, callback, verdict return, label match).

## Questions for authors' response
1. Was the September Kindnet cluster running the same image, NRI mount, and APF exemption as the July tuned bundle that recorded 9.718 s at the 5,000-Pods-per-ReplicaSet shape? If so, what changed to produce 151 s?
2. How many gateway Pods and nodes served the 35,000 sandboxes, and can you bound the denied-SYN rate reaching those nodes' evaluators before metadata convergence?
3. Can any artifact confirm whether `--fail-open` was in effect during the archived runs, and thus whether a `Running` transition could have been admitted by queue bypass rather than an evaluated verdict?
4. Why did the Kindnet cluster create Pods at 103–124/s against Cilium's 302–328/s on identical control-plane settings? Do controller-manager or API-server metrics from both clusters exist?
5. Why not compile NetworkPolicy to nftables sets/verdict maps (the factoring Section 7 attributes to Calico and Antrea) and stay in the kernel? What does userspace semantic evaluation buy that justifies bypass-on-overflow?
